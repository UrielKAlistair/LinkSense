\begin{abstract}
A Wi-Fi client in overlapping coverage must select an access point (AP)
before it can directly measure the service that each AP would provide.
Signal strength describes link quality, but not contention or load, and may
therefore favor a nearby but congested AP. This project investigates whether a
client can make a better decision using only passive observations collected
before association, without probe requests or assistance from the network.
This report defines the experimental foundation of that investigation: a
controlled ns-3 simulator that creates multi-AP deployments, records decoded
frames and physical carrier-sense activity, and replays each fixed topology once per target AP, changing only the AP the candidate joins. Every replay
of a topology therefore shares one identical pre-association observation while
yielding its own measured throughput. The simulator separates physical-topology
randomness from radio randomness and produces a structured choice-set dataset.
The resulting method supports a reproducible comparison between conventional
signal-based selection and selection based on richer passive evidence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Throughput depends on channel contention, competing stations, transmission
rates, retries, and the airtime consumed by neighboring basic service sets.
Received signal strength reflects such effects poorly if at all. Those effects do,
however, leave traces a client can hear without transmitting: the frames
themselves, and the time the medium spends busy. This work asks whether such traces are enough:
\emph{can a client choose the AP that will provide the greatest throughput
using only what its radio can passively observe before association?}

Existing load-aware methods \cite{dely2014bestap,adame2021channel} make their
load estimate after association, from AP-provided reports, or from a network
controller. A client making its first choice has none of these: only beacons
and some data frames, in which AP distance and offered traffic appear only
through their effects.

Let $\mathcal{A}=\{a_1,\ldots,a_N\}$ be the APs in one deployment and let $O$
be the candidate station's passive observation before it joins any AP. If
$y_i$ is the throughput obtained after joining $a_i$, the desired decision is

\begin{equation}
    a^* = \underset{a_i\in\mathcal{A}}{\operatorname{arg\,max}}\; y_i,
\end{equation}

using a decision function whose inputs are derived only from $O$. In a live
network only one $y_i$ is ever measurable: the station joins a single AP and
observes that AP's throughput, while the values of $y_j$ for every AP it did
not join are counterfactual. The simulator recovers all $N$ values by replaying
the same scenario once per target AP, changing only the AP the candidate joins.
Every choice set therefore carries its true $a^*$.

The replay is sound only if $O$ is identical across all $N$ runs of a scenario.
If any part of the observation varied with the AP eventually joined, a decision
function could reach the right answer through a simulation artifact rather than
through the network. This is checked rather than assumed. The assumptions that
do remain are stated next.

\section{Simulator Design}
\label{sec:sim}

The simulator is built on ns-3.45 and its Wi-Fi models \cite{ns3models}. APs
sit 30 m apart in a fixed layout: two form a pair, three an equilateral
triangle, and four, six, and eight fill $2\times2$, $3\times2$, and $4\times2$
grids. Channels 36, 40, 44, and 48 are assigned in AP-index order and reused
once a deployment exceeds four APs. Because the layout is fixed as well, co-channel
separation takes a single value per deployment size --- 30 m at eight APs, 42 m
at six, and no reuse at or below four --- which ties channel reuse to AP count.
Section~\ref{sec:next} returns to this.

Each scenario draws 2, 3, 4, 6, or 8 APs and 2, 3, or 4 background stations
per AP. A quarter of scenarios spread those stations uniformly. The
rest place one or more hotspot APs, with fewer hotspots more likely and the
count capped at $\lceil N_{\mathrm{AP}}/3\rceil$. Around those hotspots 70\% of
background stations fall in 10 m discs, and the remainder cover the
footprint. Mixing uniform and deliberately clustered regimes follows common
practice in WLAN studies \cite{wong2018daga,zhang2020placement}, and matters
here because under uniform load every AP is about equally busy and the choice
collapses back to signal strength. Each station
associates with its nearest AP, and every background station in a scenario
offers the same load, drawn uniformly at random from 1.0, 2.5, 4.0, and
6.0 Mbit/s.
Candidate positions are drawn to keep the decision non-trivial: 70\% near a
boundary between neighboring APs, 30\% at 4--16 m from a single AP. These are
sampling policies rather than radio parameters, and they are recorded with the
dataset because they set its difficulty.

Background traffic starts at $t=1$ s. Passive recording runs until $t=5.5$ s,
covering 4.5 s of loaded traffic; the candidate associates at $t=6$ s and the
run ends at $t=18$ s. The 0.5 s gap between recording and association is a
guard: ns-3 performs target-dependent polling just before a join, and without
the gap that polling would land inside the observation window. After joining,
the candidate offers 100 Mbit/s in 1250-byte packets, which keeps it backlogged
so that measured throughput reflects the service the network gives it rather
than the limits of its own traffic generator.

\subsection{Matched Counterfactual Replays}

For each sampled topology and radio seed, the experiment runs once per target
AP. AP and station positions, background traffic, the candidate's position, and
every pre-association device stay fixed; only the AP selected at $t=6$ s
changes. A \code{topologySeed} controls background-station placement and an
independent \code{rngSeed} controls fading, contention, and rate adaptation,
with five radio seeds generated per topology. Separating the two streams is
what allows stochastic radio behavior to be repeated without the physical
deployment shifting underneath it.

\subsection{Validation}

Validation runs before any model is fitted, so that a good accuracy number
cannot mask a defective data-generating process.

Three basic checks ensure that the above outlined design is actualised 
automatically: (i) Repeating a seed reproduces throughput exactly (any run can
be regenerated), (ii) Changing only the radio seed varies the outcome while every
physical position stays fixed, and (iii) The recorded frame and channel-busy
traces are byte-identical across all target-AP replays of a matched scenario
(this enforces the constant $O$ required in Section~\ref{sec:problem}).

These establish internal consistency, not that the simulator behaves like a
real network.

\section{Observation and Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:obs}

\subsection{Recording and Projection}

The candidate carries one receive-only scanner PHY per occupied channel, plus
a separate PHY reserved for association. The scanner PHYs never transmit and
never associate; the association PHY is parked on unused channel 149 until the
decision time, so the AP the candidate will eventually join cannot alter what
is heard, or consume random draws, while the observation is being collected.
At $t=5.5$ s the scanner PHYs move to the parking channel as well.

Watching every channel at once is not a claim that real clients have several
radios. It is a recording device: it captures a superset of what any single
radio could have heard, from which a realistic single-radio observation is cut
afterwards.

Two traces are recorded. The monitor-sniffer trace holds every frame a scanner
decodes, with time, frequency, BSSID, transmitter, frame category, beacon and
retry flags, length, RSSI, noise, airtime, and PHY rate. The PHY-state trace
gives the fraction of each 102.4 ms beacon interval in which carrier sense
marked the channel busy. This clear-channel-assessment (CCA) measurement
registers energy a frame-only view misses: interference, and transmissions too
distant to decode. Real chipsets expose such a counter, though whether a driver
surfaces it varies by platform.

The superset is then projected onto one passive radio. That radio rotates through the occupied channels in random order, dwelling 110 ms on each with
0.15 ms to retune, and repeats the cycle for as much of the window as it
fits, so every channel is revisited many times before the decision. Frames
and CCA intervals falling outside a dwell are discarded. A 110 ms dwell
spans just over one 102.4~ms beacon interval, so each visit yields about one
beacon from every AP on that channel. RSSI is then perturbed by uniform
error within $\pm3$ dB and quantized to 1 dB. Both steps run after ns-3 from
a recorded scan seed, so scan policy and measurement noise can be varied
without repeating a simulation.

\subsection{From Trace to Training Data}

The natural representation is the trace itself: every decoded frame in order,
carrying its arrival time, channel, BSSID and transmitter, class, flags,
length, signal, airtime, and PHY rate. It is a time series, and we train a
transformer on it directly.

The raw observation is a long sequence, and what distinguishes one AP from
another does not reside at frame granularity. Both problems that arise here 
are resolved by aggregating, which is possible only for quantities 
that can be averaged over a set of frames. 
We define a list of hand crafted features for this purpose; they are enumerated in Appendix~\ref{app:features}.

Aggregation is done per AP before the data is bucketed. Frames are attributed
to an AP by BSSID. Time is bucketed by channel visit: one bin per dwell, so
that no bin spans a retune and aggregates traffic from two channels into a
figure representing neither. A 5.5\,s window against a 110\,ms dwell gives
roughly 50 bins. Each bin carries two sets of quantities: one aggregated over
that AP's own frames, the other over all traffic the radio heard during the
visit. The second is shared by every AP in the choice set, since one radio
hears one channel at a time; a flag records whether the channel being
visited was that AP's own. A transformer attends over bins exactly as it does over frames.

Widening the bucket to the whole window removes time altogether and collapses
everything into a single row of tabular data per AP. AP-specific quantities are
aggregated over that AP's frames alone; channel-specific quantities over the
channel that AP occupies. Gradient boosting, random forests, and ridge regression
are trained on this representation.

Squashing the time axis in this way carries no comparison between options,
since it aggregates over one AP alone: the row records how busy an AP is, not whether it is busier
than its neighbors. A set of relative features is therefore engineered and
appended, restoring for the tabular models the comparison that per-AP
aggregation removed. A sequence or set model needs no such repair, because its
set stage attends across options directly.

The five radio-seed realizations of a topology are near-duplicates, so
train/test splits are cut by topology: letting two of them fall on opposite
sides would inflate every result reported below.

\section{Models, Protocol, and Results}

The dataset holds 6449 AP options across 1500 choice sets drawn from 300
topologies, a median of 4 options per decision. The best option in a set yields
42.6\,Mbit/s on average against 22.0 for one chosen at random, and the best and
worst differ by 35.5\,Mbit/s.

To bound what passive observation can provide, we train seperate models on 
the \emph{continuous} observation keeps the parallel-scanner superset in which
every channel is watched throughout. No single radio can do this in reality. 
The models of our chief interest are ones trained on the \emph{rotating}
observation, the single-radio projection of Section~\ref{sec:obs}.

Decision functions are grouped by the representation they consume. Heuristics
read the observables directly: strongest RSSI, least busy channel, and RSSI
minus channel-busy fraction. Gradient boosting, random forests, and ridge regression take the single-row vector. Transformers take the binned sequence
and the frame trace.

Comparing a transformer against models on the vector confounds architecture
with representation, because the two are given different information. Three
further encodings separate them, all derived from the binned sequence: its
average over time, a set of summary statistics per measurement, and the
sequence flattened with bin order preserved. A tabular model given the
flattened sequence receives the same per-option measurements as the
transformer. It does not receive the same information: the transformer's set
stage attends across all options in a choice set, while a tabular model scores
each row in isolation. The contrast ablation below supplies that comparison
explicitly, which is what separates cross-option attention from sequence modeling.

Both transformers use a model dimension of 48, four attention heads, and
dropout 0.1, and hold roughly $10^5$ parameters. The binned model encodes each
option's sequence with two self-attention layers over bins, reads out a learned
summary token, then compares options with two further layers over the option
set, masked so padding cannot contribute. The frame-level model cannot attend
over the raw sequence directly, since a trace runs to thousands of frames; four
learned queries per option cross-attend into it instead, which is linear rather
than quadratic in frame count. Both are trained with AdamW at $2\times10^{-3}$,
weight decay $10^{-3}$, gradient clipping at unit norm, and early stopping on
validation regret. Gradient boosting and random forests are tuned over depth,
learning rate and tree count by validation regret on each split.

Each learned model is trained under two objectives. Regression predicts
$\log(1+\text{throughput})$ in Mbit/s, so its outputs carry a scale. Ranking
learns a distribution over the options in a choice set and optimizes the
decision directly, at the cost of scores that carry no scale. Both are reported
because they need not agree on which model wins.

Every model sees the same five splits, cut by topology and stratified by AP
count. The weight initialization is held fixed across splits, so the
reported spread measures split variance alone. Metrics are top-1 accuracy
with a 0.5\,Mbit/s tie tolerance and mean regret against the oracle choice.

Table~\ref{tab:results} reports top-1 accuracy as mean and standard deviation
over the five splits. Rows are grouped by model family; differences across columns measure what
widening the observation is worth.
The frame-level model was run only on the rotating observation.


Two ablations accompany the table. Supplying every tabular model with the mean
of the other options and its own deviation from that mean hands it the
cross-option comparison the set stage performs, isolating how much of any
transformer margin is attributable to it. Shuffling the bins within each option,
leaving bin position intact, removes temporal order while preserving everything
else.

\section{Interpretation and Next Steps}
\label{sec:next}

A sequence model earns its parameters only if the ordering of the observation
carries information its summary does not. Two conditions are necessary. The
medium must vary within the observation window, and the label must depend on
what that variation implies about the period after the decision, rather than on
the mean the window already reports.

Neither holds here. Within an option's own channel, after background traffic
begins, the ratio of within-series to between-series standard deviation is 0.09
for frame rate, 0.09 for channel-busy fraction, and 0.17 for the option's own
activity. Variation between APs exceeds variation within an AP over time by an
order of magnitude. An AP looks much the same at one second as at five. This is
evidence that little is lost by averaging, not proof: stationarity does not imply
independence across bins, and a small but monotone drift would produce the
same ratio while remaining informative. The shuffle ablation is the direct
test, permuting bins within an option while leaving bin position intact.

This conclusion does not rest on the intraclass correlation. That statistic is
0.988, meaning the label is nearly determined once topology and target AP are
fixed, but it describes the reproducibility of the label across radio seeds and
implies nothing about which representation recovers it. A situation may be
fully determined by where the client stands and still be legible only from how
the medium evolves. Evidence about representation must come from the
observation, which is why the stationarity measurement is the relevant
quantity.

Table~\ref{tab:results} supports the same reading from a different direction.
Widening the observation from one rotating radio to one per channel
raises ridge regression on the flattened sequence by 0.119 while leaving the
transformer unchanged. Widening the observation from one rotating radio to one per channel raises
ridge by 0.119 and the transformer by 0.021 to 0.041. Every model on the binned
sequence improves, and the gain is ordered inversely to capacity: the weakest
model gains most. Ridge with continuous observation reaches 0.887, above the
transformer's 0.873 with rotating observation, so channel coverage substitutes
for model capacity rather than supplying information no model could otherwise
reach. Under a rotating radio roughly three-quarters of an option's bins carry
no frames from it, and a linear fit suffers more from that sparsity than
attention does.
Against a two-term heuristic that subtracts channel-busy fraction from RSSI,
its margin is roughly eleven points.

The practical conclusion is that this environment cannot discriminate between
architectures, because it does not contain the structure that would separate
them. That is a property of the simulator, and the changes below follow from
it.

\subsection{What Simulator 2 Must Change}

The environment must become non-stationary on a timescale shorter than the
observation window, and visibly so in a passive trace: load that arrives and
departs, stations that join and leave, interference that appears part-way
through. Equally important, the label must stop being an average over a long
horizon. A twelve-second mean of a process that is already stationary is
predicted best by the window mean; a near-horizon label, measured over the
second or two immediately after the decision, is what makes a trend worth
extrapolating.

Four smaller changes follow from the present work. Channel assignment by AP index over a fixed layout leaves co-channel separation
with no variance and ties reuse to deployment size. This is a leakage path, not
only a realism defect: the number of options in a choice set determines the AP
count in 91.8\% of cases, and the AP count fixes the channel plan exactly, so a
model can infer the reuse regime from the size of its input rather than from
radio evidence. The guard of Section~\ref{sec:problem} does not cover this,
since it checks only that the observation is independent of which AP is
joined. Sampling the channel assignment would break the dependence. AP
spacing of 30\,m pairs an obstructed-indoor path-loss exponent with an
open-field geometry. The BSS Load element, which a passive client reads from a
single beacon, is absent and is the strongest baseline this comparison lacks.
And the restriction to complete silence, rather than to the pre-association
period, could be relaxed to admit probe requests.

\subsection{Ranking Versus Regression}

The two objectives are not equally testable outside a simulator, and the
difference favors the one this work treats as secondary.

Ranking asks which AP is best. Establishing ground truth for that answer
requires joining each option under identical conditions, which is exactly what the matched replay of Section~\ref{sec:sim} provides and what no field
measurement can. A ranking model's output is therefore not easily verifiable per
decision in the field. This work reports both objectives because the simulator supports both. A
successor aimed at deployment should treat throughput prediction as the primary
target, and derive the choice from it, rather than optimizing the choice
directly and inheriting a claim that cannot be checked.
