\begin{abstract}
A Wi-Fi client in overlapping coverage must select an access point (AP)
before it can directly measure the service that each AP would provide.
Signal strength describes link quality, but not contention or load, and may
therefore favor a nearby but congested AP. This project investigates whether a
client can make a better decision using only passive observations collected
before association without assistance from the network.

We define a controlled ns-3 simulator of multi-AP deployments to investigate this.
The simulation of each topology of devices is replayed n times to a candidate STA
to obtain the throughputs offered by each AP. 

\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Throughput depends on channel contention, competing stations, transmission
rates, retries, and the airtime consumed by neighboring basic service sets.
Received signal strength reflects such effects poorly if at all. Those effects do,
however, leave traces a client can hear without transmitting: the frames
themselves, and the time the medium spends busy. 
This project asks whether such traces are enough: \emph{can a client choose 
the AP that will provide the greatest throughput using only what its radio 
can passively observe before association?}

Existing load-aware methods \cite{dely2014bestap,adame2021channel} make their
load estimate after association, from AP-provided reports, or from a network
controller. A client making its first choice has none of these: only beacons
and some data frames, in which AP distance and offered traffic appear only
through their effects.

Let $\mathcal{A}=\{a_1,\ldots,a_{N_{\mathrm{AP}}}\}$ be the APs in one deployment and let $O$
be the candidate station's passive observation before it joins any AP. If
$y_i$ is the throughput obtained after joining $a_i$, the desired decision is

\begin{equation}
    a^* = \underset{a_i\in\mathcal{A}}{\operatorname{arg\,max}}\; y_i,
\end{equation}

using a decision function whose inputs are derived only from $O$. 

We tackle this problem by predicting the value of $y_i$ for each $a_i$.

It is worth noting that models that simply output a ranking of the available choices are
viable as well. In the simulated data we ensure that we have true counterfactuals 
by deliberately verifying $O$ to be identical across all $N_{\mathrm{AP}}$ runs of a given scenario. 
In a live network however, only one $y_i$ is ever measurable: the station joins a single 
AP and observes that AP's throughput, while the values of $y_j$ for every AP it did not 
join are not easily recoverable. For broader applicability, and adaptation to reality, 
choice models are not investigated extensively here.

\section{Simulator Design and Assumptions}
\label{sec:sim}

\subsection{Simulation Environment}

The simulator is built on ns-3.45 and its Wi-Fi models \cite{ns3models}.
The deployment is static and single-floor with no walls, the environment TGax
assumes for its indoor small-BSS scenario \cite{tgax2016scenarios}. 
All stations are immobile and remain at their seeded positions. 
Radios are homogeneous IEEE 802.11n devices at 5\,GHz on 20\,MHz channels 
with Minstrel-HT rate control \cite{ns3models}. 

Every radio is an ns-3 \code{SpectrumWifiPhy} attached to one shared
\code{MultiModelSpectrumChannel}, on which a log-distance loss (exponent 3.0,
with 46.6777\,dB of reference loss at 1 m) is followed by Nakagami fading.

\code{SpectrumWifiPhy} is chosen over the simpler \code{YansWifiPhy} as the Yans model 
simulates exactly zero cross channel interference by treating each channel as 
an isolated layer. If \code{YansWifiPhy} is used, the channel occupancy given by CCA 
measurements becomes a powerful signal for models in a way that does not reflect reality.

\subsection{Count, Topology and Channel occupancy of APs}

\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \input{arch_figures_tikz/fig_deployment}
  \caption{An example simulated deployment.}
  \label{fig:deployment}
\end{figure}

Each scenario first draws 2, 3, 4, 6, or 8 APs. They sit on a triangular
lattice 20 m apart, following \cite{tgax2016scenarios}, where it is reasoned
that such a topology keeps the nearest-neighbor distance from varing with the number of
APs. APs are confined to two rows of the lattice with the bottom row having 
$min(2, \lceil\frac{N_{\mathrm{AP}}}{2}\rceil)$ APs. 

Each AP then draws a channel uniformly at random from channels 
36, 40, 44, and 48. Random assignment biases the data toward more in-channel 
contention and interference which could potentially make the problem harder than if 
we plan out the channels to be minimally interfering. However, TGax sets precedent
for random assignment across four channels in its enterprise scenario, and across 
the non-overlapping set in its residential one \cite{tgax2016scenarios}, 
so this is the strategy adopted.

\subsection{Count, Topology of background STAs}

The total number of stations is decided by independently drawing a count of 3, 4, or 5 
$N_{\mathrm{AP}}$ times and summing them.

A quarter of scenarios spread the stations uniformly. 
The rest declare one or more APs as "hotspots" around which stations are clustered. 
Such mixing of uniform and deliberately clustered regimes follows common practice in WLAN studies
\cite{wong2018daga,zhang2020placement}, and is of particular import here because under uniform
load every AP is about equally busy and the problem we examine collapses to signal
strength in a fashion uncharacteristic of reality.

We draw the number of hotspots at random with fewer hotspots being more likely
and the count capped at $\lceil N_{\mathrm{AP}}/3\rceil$. The area around a hotspot AP,
a 10 m disc around it, is assigned a probability density 4 times that of non-hotspot 
areas. Each station's position is drawn from this distribution. 

\subsection{Association and Load of background STAs}

Each station associates with the AP whose received power is highest. This
power is calculated with a log-distance term, then perturbed by a shadowing
term: a random sample from a Gaussian of 5\,dB standard deviation. Without
the perturbation, stations form exact Voronoi partitions, which no real
deployment does; 5\,dB is the indoor shadowing deviation TGax specifies
\cite{tgax2016scenarios}. 

Every station then draws its own offered load, independently, from a log-normal
distribution with median 2.25\,Mbit/s and shape 0.9, capped at 25\,Mbit/s, for a
mean of 3.37\,Mbit/s. Traffic volumes are log-normal rather than Gaussian
\cite{lognormal2020}, and the right skew is what puts most stations on a light
load and a few on a heavy one without needing a mixture. The quartiles span the
mix an AP actually carries, from voice and browsing up through
high-definition video.

\subsection{Placement of candidate STA}

The candidate station is placed uniformly at random over the same area as the
background stations: the AP footprint plus a 10 m border.

\subsection{Data Capture}

Background traffic starts at $t=0.5$ s. Each station's arrivals are Poisson at
its own data rate, and its first packet is delayed by a phase drawn uniformly
over one mean interval, without which, stations that share an AP and a rate
transmit in lockstep for the whole run.

Passive recording runs from $t=2$ s to $t=8$ s, covering 6\,s of loaded
traffic. It starts 1.5\,s after the traffic because Minstrel-HT needs about that
long to converge, and until it does every scenario carries the same opening
burst of retransmissions. 

The candidate starts joining at $t=8$ s, the moment recording ends. After joining, 
the candidate offers 100\,Mbit/s in 1250-byte packets until the
run ends at $t=20$ s. That rate keeps it backlogged, so its measured throughput
reflects the service the network gives it rather than the limit of its own
traffic generator.

\subsection{Matched Counterfactual Replays}

For each sampled topological arrangement of the candidate + environment, 
the experiment runs once per target AP. 
AP and station positions, background traffic, the candidate's position, and
every pre-association device stay fixed; only the AP selected at $t=8$ s
changes. A \code{topologySeed} fixes the deployment: station placement, channel
selection, and the load offered by each station. Independently, \code{candidateSeed}
fixes the candidate STA's posistion, and \code{rngSeed} controls all other randomness 
such as fading, contention, rate adaptation, and arrival times of background packets.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:obs}

\subsection{Recording and Projection}

A deployed client scans across all channels that host APs in a rotating fashion.
The simulation records more than any deployed client can be expected to observe
by recording activity on every occupied channel simultaneously. We then project 
this complete record down to what a single scan would see.

Specifically, the candidate STA carries one receive-only scanner PHY per
occupied channel, plus a separate PHY reserved for association. The scanner
PHYs never transmit and never associate; the association PHY is parked on
unused channel 149 until the decision time. At $t=8$ s the scanner PHYs move to
the parking channel, and the assocation PHY moves into the channel of the AP it 
intends to assocate with.

Two traces are recorded. The monitor-sniffer trace holds every frame a scanner
decodes, with the start and end of the transmission carrying it, frequency,
BSSID, transmitter, frame category, beacon and retry flags, length, RSSI,
airtime, and PHY rate. Frames aggregated into one transmission share their start
and end timestamps. 

The PHY-state trace gives the fraction of each 1\,ms interval in which carrier sense
marked the channel busy. This clear-channel-assessment (CCA) measurement
registers energy a frame-only view misses: interference, and transmissions too
distant to decode. Both thresholds are left at the ns-3 defaults: $-62$ dBm of
received energy from any source, or $-82$ dBm from a signal whose preamble the
PHY recognizes. Most driver stacks expose an equivalent channel-busy counter, 
though availability varies by platform.

The projection onto one passive radio is done as follows: 
One radio is assumed to rotate through the occupied channels in a certain 
randomly decided order, dwelling 110\,ms on each with 0.15\,ms to retune, 
and repeats the cycle for as much of the observation window as possible. 
A frame is kept only if its whole transmission falls inside a dwell after retuning: 
CCA intervals outside a dwell are discarded. A 110\,ms dwell spans just over one 102.4\,ms beacon 
interval, so each visit yields about one beacon from every AP on that channel. 

RSSI is then perturbed by uniform error within $\pm3$ dB and quantized to 1\,dB. 
Both steps run after ns-3 from a recorded scan seed, so scan policy and 
measurement noise can be varied without repeating a simulation.

\subsection{Training Data}

The candidate is placed at five positions in each of the 1{,}000 deployments.
Seven of the resulting 5{,}000 scans heard beacons from fewer than two APs and
were dropped, having no choice to offer; each of the rest contributes one row
per discovered AP.

Training splits are drawn by topology rather than by scan.

\section{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:model}

The model adapts a pretrained language model to this problem following the 
structure of NetLLM~\cite{wu2024netllm}: A learned encoder turns the
observation into a sequence of vectors, a frozen decoder-only backbone reads
that sequence as input embeddings, and only low-rank
adapters~\cite{hu2022lora} on its attention projections are trained.

Every model in this section scores the \emph{discovered APs} of a scan: those
whose beacons it decoded at least once. An AP never heard leaves nothing in the
observation to read from, so the $\arg\max$ of Section~\ref{sec:problem} is in
practice taken over the discovered APs rather than over every AP in the
deployment. This is a limit of passive observation rather than of the models,
and it costs little, since an AP too distant for its beacons to arrive is
unlikely to be the one that would have served best.

\subsection{Embedding}

The observation window contains several thousand frames, and carrier sense
datapoints making attending over the scan frame by frame impractical. Further,
several meaningful signals lay as aggregates or counts which would not be best 
captured by such a model.

Thus, we undertake the following process to vectorize our observation:
First, the scan is cut into bins over time. Each bin is chosen to be of 110\,ms, 
such that it coincides with channel dwells, keeping same-channel frames together. 
For each bin, we emit several \emph{cells}. One cell is emitted per discovered AP: 
each of these only contains frames that carry its BSSID. We also emit one channel 
cell with all the frames of the dwell.

The model then learns a representation for each cell by allowing frames within the 
cell to attend amongst themselves. Frames carry 10 numerical values and a 
categorical flag; 9 numbers and the category are embedded into a 48-dimensional space 
with a 2 layer MLP. 
Non-linearity is needed here to capture relations between the frame characteristics, 
some of which are boolean, for attention to better relate frames to each other. 
The tenth value, time, is positionally encoded. The time from the start of the dwell 
is encoded as 8 sine-cosine pairs, with periods falling geometrically from 220\,ms to 
0.2\,ms~\cite{vaswani2017}. 
The fast end is about the airtime of one frame, below which there is nothing to 
distinguish; the slow end is twice the longest gap a cell can hold, so that the
sine-cosine pair turns less than half a circle over any gap within a dwell and 
orders it unambiguously. The 8 pairs are appended to the aforementioned embedding, 
creating an overall embedding dimension of 64 for each frame.

Setting position beside the content rather than adding it in~\cite{ke2021tupe}
is what keeps the pairwise structure intact: the dot product of two frames' 16
position columns depends only on the gap between their start times, so the
attention layer that follows can read how far apart two frames were without a learned
layer having had to preserve the encoding. 

The attended frames are then reduced to a single vector. We take two reductions
and concatenate them: the mean over the cell's frames, and the output of one
learned query attending over them, the pooling of the Set
Transformer~\cite{lee2019set}. The mean reports the cell as a whole, while the
query is free to weight a few frames above the rest. A cell hands over at most
512 frames --- a random subset while training, an even stride at evaluation ---
which bounds an attention cost that grows with the square of the frame count;
the cap binds on 0.5\% of cells.

Two exact summaries join the pooled vector. The first is 16 aggregates taken
over every frame the cell held, including those the cap dropped.
Table~\ref{tab:inputs} lists the quantities that are aggregated here.
Such counts and totals cannot be reported by the pooled set of vectors 
and are thus directly given to the model. The second summary belongs to channel 
cells alone: the 109 carrier-sense samples of the dwell, one per millisecond, 
read by a strided one-dimensional convolution. (One CCA sample is lost to 
radio retune)

A final MLP maps the concatenation of the summaries and the pooled vector 
to a single 64 dimensional vector leaving the encoder. (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). 
Both an AP and a channel cell share the frame embedding and the self-attention,
but each keeps its own pooling query and its own output MLP, since only 
channel cells carry CCA signals, and an aggregate such as beacon
level does not mean the same thing on the two and should not afforded the same 
mapping. A cell that heard nothing has nothing to pool and takes a learned 
vector in place of both reductions.

The resulting cell vector, although containing a summary of all its frames, mostly 
lacks identifiers to distinguish itself or relate to other cells. Three learned 
embeddings are added to every cell vector: the channel it was heard on, 
a bool for whether it is an AP cell or a channel cell and the dwell
it covers as an index into the sweep. This leaves just the AP identity to be 
embedded and we take two approaches at this juncture:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Declare one AP as the target AP whose throughput we are interested in.
  We now embed the identity relative to this AP: \code{TARGET\_AP} for the target's
  own cells, \code{CO\_CHANNEL\_AP} for an AP sharing its channel, and
  \code{OTHER\_AP} for the rest. This means that inference is effectively 
  run once per target AP, with a different set of identities added onto the 
  cell embeddings.

  \item  Directly embed the AP's numerical identity. This needs one hot encoding 
  of the AP's number and then mapping it to a vector space, so it fixes the maximum
  number of APs the model handles at once. However, this model can predict the
  throughputs of all the APs in one inference, since the embedding is absolute and 
  not relative.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Backbone}
\label{sec:backbone}

The tagged cell vectors attend across the scan so that a cell describing one
AP can incorporate the activity of other APs and channels. We consider two
backbones for this step.

The first is a transformer encoder trained from scratch, with two blocks of
four-head self-attention at width 64. Each block normalises its input before
attention, adds the attended values to the input, then applies a feed-forward
MLP with another normalisation and residual connection. Every cell can attend
to every observed cell; dwell order is supplied by the tags.

The second follows the adaptation of a pretrained language model described
above. Linear projections map the cell vectors from 64 to 2048 dimensions,
then back after Llama-3.2-1B has processed them. The backbone weights remain
frozen, with rank-8 LoRA adapters on the query and value projections. Its
attention mask permits cells to attend within their own dwell and to earlier
dwells. Cells in the same dwell receive the same rotary position, since their
order within that dwell carries no meaning~\cite{su2024roformer}.

The target-AP model runs the backbone separately for each AP, because changing
the target changes the role tags on its input. The joint-AP model runs it once
for the scan. AP identity codes in this model are assigned randomly within each
sample and shared between an AP's cells and its readout; they provide a way to
match observations without identifying an AP across deployments.

\subsection{Decoder}
\label{sec:decoder}

The backbone returns one vector per cell, while we need one prediction per AP.
To obtain it, we construct a query row from the AP's window descriptors
(Table~\ref{tab:inputs}). A two-layer MLP projects them to 64 dimensions, to
which we add a learned query vector and the AP's channel tag. In the joint
model, the row also receives the identity tag carried by that AP's cells.

These rows pass through two decoder blocks. Each block lets the rows attend
to the backbone's cell vectors, using the rows as queries and the cells as keys
and values, then applies a feed-forward MLP. Both operations have normalisation
and residual connections. In the joint model, self-attention between AP rows
precedes cross-attention, allowing each prediction to use what the other rows
have learned. The target model has one row per backbone pass and omits this step.

A final MLP maps each row to a mean and log variance of standardised
$\log(1+y_i)$, where $y_i$ is throughput in Mbit/s. Training begins with squared
error on the mean and then uses Gaussian negative log likelihood to fit both
outputs. The predicted means order the APs for selection.

\section{Architecture Choices}
\label{sec:choices}

\subsection{Adding or Concatenating Identity}

The embedding above concatenates time with frame content, but adds identity
to cell content. Both choices use a total width of 64. Concatenation reserves
coordinates for each component; addition lets both use the full width, at the
cost of superposing them~\cite{vaswani2017}.

For cells, reserving 16 dimensions for tags and projecting content into the
remaining 48 performed worse in the ablations than adding the tags to a
64-dimensional content vector. This supports addition at the cell level.
Compressing a cell's frame and aggregate summaries into 48 dimensions may
lose useful information. The comparison also changes the width available to
the tags and how they meet the content, so it does not isolate that explanation.

The separation also lasts only as long as subsequent layers preserve it.
The value and output projections can write attended content into any
coordinate, as can the feed-forward layers. After the first residual update,
the reserved coordinates may contain both identity and content.

The sinusoidal argument at the frame level needs a similar qualification.
For a time encoding $p(\tau)$ with sine-cosine pairs at frequencies $\omega_k$,
\begin{equation}
 p(\tau_i)^\top p(\tau_j)
 = \sum_k \cos\!\left(\omega_k(\tau_i-\tau_j)\right).
\end{equation}
This dot product depends only on the time difference. Attention, however,
compares projected queries and keys. Writing their maps from the positional
coordinates as $U_Q$ and $U_K$, the positional term is
$p(\tau_i)^\top U_Q^\top U_K p(\tau_j)$, which need not retain that property.
Concatenation alone neither constrains these projections nor removes the
content--position cross terms. TUPE~\cite{ke2021tupe} treats content and position
with separate attention terms; reserving input coordinates does not implement
that separation.

Addition offers a different allocation of the same width: all 64 coordinates
can represent frame content, and time can use 32 sine-cosine pairs rather than
8. More pairs sample the chosen frequency range more densely, though that
need not improve the task. In the V1000 fold-0 comparison, additive frame
position gave mean throughput regret of 0.461\,Mbit/s against 0.481\,Mbit/s for
concatenation, with additive cell tags in both runs. Regret measures the
throughput lost by selecting the predicted best AP rather than the true best.
This single-fold difference does not establish a reliable advantage. We retain
concatenation in the frame description while this choice remains open.

\subsection{Identity in the Attention Logits}

Two cells' channel tags already say whether they share a channel. We can also
supply that relationship directly to attention. In the custom encoder, each
head can learn a scalar for each channel separation, measured in 20\,MHz steps,
and add it to the scaled query--key score before softmax. The decoder can use
its own channel-separation table and an additional scalar indicating whether
the cell belongs to the AP being scored. These are learned preferences rather
than masks: every observed cell remains available to attention.

T5~\cite{raffel2020t5} and Swin~\cite{liu2021swin} use learned relative-position
biases in attention, while Graphormer~\cite{ying2021graphormer} supplies graph
relationships through such biases. Here, however, the relationships are also
present in the tags. This raises the question of whether the explicit biases
provide a useful constraint or duplicate something attention can learn.

To see the overlap, write an input as content plus tag, $x_i=c_i+t_i$.
Let $W_Q$ and $W_K$ be one head's query and key maps. Temporarily omitting
normalisation, projection biases and the attention scale, set
$M=W_Q^\top W_K$. Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 q_i^\top k_j ={}& c_i^\top M c_j + c_i^\top M t_j \\
                 &+ t_i^\top M c_j + t_i^\top M t_j.
\end{split}
\label{eq:tag_attention}
\end{equation}
The last term assigns a score to a pair of tags independently of content.
It can therefore express preferences such as matching identities, which are
also supplied by an explicit bias. In cross-attention the same expansion
applies with row content and tags on the query side and cell content and tags
on the key side.

This overlap does not establish equivalence. A tag-derived table is constrained
by the tag vectors and the head's projections; an explicit table directly ties
all pairs with the same relationship to one parameter. Further, our blocks
normalise content and tags together before projecting them. Their interaction
therefore depends on the content, and later layers also receive attended
values. Equation~\ref{eq:tag_attention} motivates the comparison rather than
proving that either mechanism is redundant.

\subsection{Tag Direction and Magnitude}

In the earlier runs, cell content grew to lengths around 24--25 while the
small Gaussian-initialised tags remained much smaller. Across the ablations,
the tags changed little from their initial values. This suggests that their
initial scale may determine how readily attention uses identity, but does not
by itself show that their directions need no learning.

We can separate these questions by fixing the directions. Let $u_a$ be a unit
code for identity $a$, with $u_a^\top u_b=0$ for distinct identities, and write
$t_a=\alpha u_a$. In the unnormalised expansion, the tag--tag term becomes
\begin{equation}
 t_a^\top M t_b = \alpha^2 u_a^\top M u_b.
\end{equation}
The cross terms scale linearly with $\alpha$. Thus a small initial magnitude
can weaken the purely relational contribution more strongly than the mixed
ones. Its gradient also depends on the learned projections and, in the actual
model, normalisation. The quadratic term alone does not imply that gradient
descent cannot increase the magnitude.

For this experiment we hold content at unit L2 length before adding tags.
LayerNorm with trainable scale and offset does not impose that constraint:
its output length can change during training. Fixed content length gives the
tag magnitude a stable reference, while the MLP still learns the content's
direction.

Cell type, channel and AP identity receive mutually orthonormal codes from
one fixed basis. Dwell receives a fixed sinusoidal encoding in the remaining
subspace, preserving a notion of temporal separation. Each of these four
codebooks has one trainable scalar magnitude shared by its entries, initially
0.5. The same channel and AP codes are used in the decoder rows, whose content
is also normalised to unit length. Fixing the codes still leaves the query and
key projections free to learn which relationships matter.

\subsection{Biases and Tag Learning}

An explicit bias receives a gradient directly from its attention logit.
Changing a tag magnitude acts through the projections, normalisation and
several terms of the score. The bias may therefore make useful relationships
easier to learn. It may also let the model rely on those relationships without
learning to use the tags. Better performance with a bias and little movement
in the tags are compatible with either explanation; neither establishes that
the bias obstructs learning.

We test the fixed-code model in four configurations: no explicit biases,
encoder bias only, both encoder and decoder biases, and decoder bias only.
All four use fold 0 of V1000, the same initial seed and training schedule.
These runs ask whether magnitude learning makes the explicit biases
unnecessary, and whether their effect differs between cell interaction and AP
readout. They do not compare fixed directions against learned directions under
otherwise identical conditions. That comparison, and repetition across folds,

fractions are normalized by the time actually spent listening on the relevant
channel, not by the observation window. A \code{log1p} suffix marks a count or
