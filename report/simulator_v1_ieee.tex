\begin{abstract}
A Wi-Fi client in overlapping coverage must select an access point (AP)
before it can directly measure the service that each AP would provide.
Signal strength describes link quality, but not contention or load, and may
therefore favor a nearby but congested AP. This project investigates whether a
client can make a better decision using only passive observations collected
before association without assistance from the network.

We define a controlled ns-3 simulator of multi-AP deployments to investigate this.
The simulation of each topology of devices is replayed n times to a candidate STA
to obtain the throughputs offered by each AP. 

\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Throughput depends on channel contention, competing stations, transmission
rates, retries, and the airtime consumed by neighboring basic service sets.
Received signal strength reflects such effects poorly if at all. Those effects do,
however, leave traces a client can hear without transmitting: the frames
themselves, and the time the medium spends busy. 
This project asks whether such traces are enough: \emph{can a client choose 
the AP that will provide the greatest throughput using only what its radio 
can passively observe before association?}

Existing load-aware methods \cite{dely2014bestap,adame2021channel} make their
load estimate after association, from AP-provided reports, or from a network
controller. A client making its first choice has none of these: only beacons
and some data frames, in which AP distance and offered traffic appear only
through their effects.

Let $\mathcal{A}=\{a_1,\ldots,a_{N_{\mathrm{AP}}}\}$ be the APs in one deployment and let $O$
be the candidate station's passive observation before it joins any AP. If
$y_i$ is the throughput obtained after joining $a_i$, the desired decision is

\begin{equation}
    a^* = \underset{a_i\in\mathcal{A}}{\operatorname{arg\,max}}\; y_i,
\end{equation}

using a decision function whose inputs are derived only from $O$. 

We tackle this problem by predicting the value of $y_i$ for each $a_i$.

It is worth noting that models that simply output a ranking of the available choices are
viable as well. In the simulated data we ensure that we have true counterfactuals 
by deliberately verifying $O$ to be identical across all $N_{\mathrm{AP}}$ runs of a given scenario. 
In a live network however, only one $y_i$ is ever measurable: the station joins a single 
AP and observes that AP's throughput, while the values of $y_j$ for every AP it did not 
join are not easily recoverable. For broader applicability, and adaptation to reality, 
choice models are not investigated extensively here.

\section{Simulator Design and Assumptions}
\label{sec:sim}

\subsection{Simulation Environment}

The simulator is built on ns-3.45 and its Wi-Fi models \cite{ns3models}.
The deployment is static and single-floor with no walls, the environment TGax
assumes for its indoor small-BSS scenario \cite{tgax2016scenarios}. 
All stations are immobile and remain at their seeded positions. 
Radios are homogeneous IEEE 802.11n devices at 5\,GHz on 20\,MHz channels 
with Minstrel-HT rate control \cite{ns3models}. 

Every radio is an ns-3 \code{SpectrumWifiPhy} attached to one shared
\code{MultiModelSpectrumChannel}, on which a log-distance loss (exponent 3.0,
with 46.6777\,dB of reference loss at 1 m) is followed by Nakagami fading.

\code{SpectrumWifiPhy} is chosen over the simpler \code{YansWifiPhy} as the Yans model 
simulates exactly zero cross channel interference by treating each channel as 
an isolated layer. If \code{YansWifiPhy} is used, the channel occupancy given by CCA 
measurements becomes a powerful signal for models in a way that does not reflect reality.

\subsection{Count, Topology and Channel occupancy of APs}

\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \input{arch_figures_tikz/fig_deployment}
  \caption{An example simulated deployment.}
  \label{fig:deployment}
\end{figure}

Each scenario first draws 2, 3, 4, 6, or 8 APs. They sit on a triangular
lattice 20 m apart, following \cite{tgax2016scenarios}. This keeps the nearest-neighbor 
distance from varying with the number of APs. APs are confined to two rows of the lattice 
with the bottom row having $max(2, \lceil\frac{N_{\mathrm{AP}}}{2}\rceil)$ APs. 

Each AP then draws a channel uniformly at random from channels 
36, 40, 44, and 48. Random assignment biases the data toward more in-channel 
contention and interference which could potentially make the problem harder than if 
we plan out the channels to be minimally interfering. However, TGax sets precedent
for random assignment across four channels in its enterprise scenario, and across 
the non-overlapping set in its residential one \cite{tgax2016scenarios}, 
so this is the strategy adopted.

\subsection{Count, Topology of background STAs}

The total number of stations is decided by independently drawing a count of 3, 4, or 5 
$N_{\mathrm{AP}}$ times and summing them.

A quarter of scenarios spread the stations uniformly. 
The rest declare one or more APs as ``hotspots'' around which stations are clustered. 
Such mixing of uniform and deliberately clustered regimes follows common practice in WLAN studies
\cite{wong2018daga,zhang2020placement}, and is of particular import here because under uniform
load every AP is about equally busy and the problem we examine collapses to signal
strength in a fashion uncharacteristic of reality.

We draw the number of hotspots at random with fewer hotspots being more likely
and the count capped at $\lceil N_{\mathrm{AP}}/3\rceil$. The area around a hotspot AP,
a 10 m disc around it, is assigned a probability density 4 times that of non-hotspot 
areas. Each station's position is drawn from this distribution. 

\subsection{Association and Load of background STAs}

Each station associates with the AP whose received power is highest. This
power is calculated with a log-distance term, then perturbed by a shadowing
term: a random sample from a Gaussian of 5\,dB standard deviation. Without
the perturbation, stations form exact Voronoi partitions, which no real
deployment does; 5\,dB is the indoor shadowing deviation TGax specifies
\cite{tgax2016scenarios}. 

Every station then draws its own offered load, independently, from a log-normal
distribution with median 2.25\,Mbit/s and shape 0.9, capped at 25\,Mbit/s, for a
mean of 3.37\,Mbit/s. Traffic volumes are log-normal rather than Gaussian
\cite{lognormal2020}, and the right skew is what puts most stations on a light
load and a few on a heavy one without needing a mixture. Its quartiles, 
about 1.2 and 4.1\,Mbit/s, cover browsing and standard-definition streaming, 
and the upper tail reaches high-definition video.


\subsection{Placement of candidate STA}

The candidate station is placed uniformly at random over the same area as the
background stations: the AP footprint plus a 10 m border.

\subsection{Data Capture}

Background traffic starts at $t=0.5$ s. Each station's arrivals are Poisson at
its own data rate, and its first packet is delayed by a phase drawn uniformly
over one mean interval.

Passive recording runs from $t=2$ s to $t=8$ s, covering 6\,s of loaded
traffic. It starts 1.5\,s after the traffic because Minstrel-HT needs about that
long to converge, and until it does every scenario carries the same opening
burst of retransmissions. 

The candidate starts joining at $t=8$ s, the moment recording ends. After joining, 
the candidate offers 100\,Mbit/s in 1250-byte packets until the
run ends at $t=20$ s. That rate keeps it backlogged, so its measured throughput
reflects the service the network gives it rather than the limit of its own
traffic generator. We define the throughput label as the mean rate offered from the 
moment of association to t=20 s; a candidate that never associates scores 0.

\subsection{Matched Counterfactual Replays}

For each sampled topological arrangement of the candidate + environment, 
the experiment runs once per target AP. 
AP and station positions, background traffic, the candidate's position, and
every pre-association device stay fixed; only the AP selected at $t=8$ s
changes. A \code{topologySeed} fixes the deployment: station placement, channel
selection, and the load offered by each station. Independently, \code{candidateSeed}
fixes the candidate STA's position, and \code{rngSeed} controls all other randomness 
such as fading, contention, rate adaptation, and arrival times of background packets.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:obs}

\subsection{Recording and Projection}

A deployed client scans across all available channels in a rotating fashion.
The simulation records more than any deployed client can be expected to observe
by recording activity on every occupied channel simultaneously. We then project 
this complete record down to what a single scan would see.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \input{arch_figures_tikz/fig_scan}
  \caption{The all-channel recording projected onto one passive radio.}
  \label{fig:scan}
\end{figure}

Specifically, the candidate STA carries one receive-only scanner PHY per
occupied channel, plus a separate PHY reserved for association. The scanner
PHYs never transmit and never associate; the association PHY is parked on
unused channel 149 until the decision time. At $t=8$ s the scanner PHYs move to
the parking channel, and the association PHY moves into the channel of the AP it 
intends to associate with.

Two traces are recorded. The monitor-sniffer trace holds every frame a scanner
decodes, with the start and end of the transmission carrying it, frequency,
BSSID, transmitter, frame category, beacon and retry flags, length, RSSI,
airtime, and PHY rate. Frames aggregated into one transmission share their start
and end timestamps. 

The PHY-state trace gives the fraction of each 1\,ms interval in which carrier sense
marked the channel busy. This clear-channel-assessment (CCA) measurement
registers energy a frame-only view misses: interference, and transmissions too
distant to decode. Both thresholds are left at the ns-3 defaults: $-62$ dBm of
received energy from any source, or $-82$ dBm from a signal whose preamble the
PHY recognizes. Most driver stacks expose an equivalent channel-busy counter, 
though availability varies by platform.

The projection onto one passive radio is done as follows: 
One radio is assumed to rotate through the available channels in a certain 
randomly decided order, dwelling 110\,ms on each with 0.15\,ms to retune, 
and repeats the cycle for as much of the observation window as possible. Only an integer number 
of all-channel cycles are retained in the dataset, and the last few dwells may be
excluded to this effect. A frame is retained in the dataset only if its whole transmission falls 
inside a dwell after retuning: CCA intervals outside a dwell are discarded as well. 
A 110\,ms dwell spans just over one 102.4\,ms beacon interval, so each visit yields about 
one beacon from every AP on that channel. Finally, the RSSI of each frame is quantized to 1\,dB when 
creating the dataset so as to match practical precision.

\subsection{Training Data}

The corpus used in the making of this report simulated 1{,}000 deployments,  
placing the candidate at five positions in each of them. Seven of the resulting 
5{,}000 \textit{``scans''} heard beacons from fewer than two APs and were dropped, 
having no choice to offer.

Training splits of this dataset were drawn by topology rather than by scan. 
The five scans of a deployment differ only in where the candidate stands:

in training would score the model on something close to what it was fitted on. 
Deployments are dealt 60\% to training, 20\% to validation and 20\% to test, 
each AP count dealt separately so that no split ends up with a disproportionate share 
of the largest or smallest deployments.

\section{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:model}

The model adapts a pretrained language model to this problem following the 
structure of NetLLM~\cite{wu2024netllm}: A learned encoder turns the
observation into a sequence of vectors, a frozen decoder-only backbone reads
that sequence as input embeddings, and only low-rank
adapters~\cite{hu2022lora} on its attention projections are trained.

Every model in this section scores the \emph{discovered APs} of a scan: those
whose beacons it decoded at least once. An AP never heard leaves nothing in the
observation to read from, so the $\arg\max$ of Section~\ref{sec:problem} is in
practice taken over the discovered APs rather than over every AP in the
deployment. This is a limit of passive observation rather than of the models,
and it costs little, since an AP too distant for its beacons to arrive is
unlikely to be the one that would have served best.

\subsection{Embedding}

The observation window contains several thousand frames and carrier sense
datapoints, making attending over the scan frame by frame impractical. Further,
several meaningful signals lie as aggregates or counts which would not be best 
captured by such a model.

Thus, we undertake the following process to vectorize our observation:
First, the scan is cut into bins over time. Each bin is chosen to be of 110\,ms, 
such that it coincides with channel dwells, keeping same-channel frames together. 
For each bin, we emit several \emph{cells}. One cell is emitted per discovered AP
on that dwell's channel: each of these only contains frames that carry its BSSID. 
We also emit one channel cell with all the frames of the dwell.

The model then learns a representation for each cell by allowing frames within the 
cell to attend amongst themselves. A maximum of 512 frames are used for this purpose 
to limit quadratic attention costs contributed by a few outlier cells. 

Frames carry 10 numerical values and a 
categorical flag; 9 numbers and the category are embedded into a 48-dimensional space 
with a 2 layer MLP. 
Non-linearity is needed here to capture relations between the frame characteristics, 
some of which are boolean, for attention to better relate frames to each other. 
The tenth value, time, is positionally encoded. The time from the start of the dwell 
is encoded as 8 sine-cosine pairs, with periods falling geometrically from 220\,ms to 
0.2\,ms~\cite{vaswani2017}. 
The fast end is about the airtime of one frame, below which there is nothing to 
distinguish; the slow end is twice the longest gap a cell can hold, so that the
sine-cosine pair turns less than half a circle over any gap within a dwell and 
orders it unambiguously. The 8 pairs are appended to the aforementioned embedding, 
creating an overall embedding dimension of 64 for each frame.

The original transformer \textit{adds} the positional encoding to the token embedding, rather than 
concatenate, so the position and the content share all coordinates~\cite{vaswani2017}. 
This has a notable advantage that both content and position are able to use the full
dimensionality of the embedding, allowing for greater expressibility of both of them. 
The drawback with this approach is that content and position are mixed into the same values
and separating out that information is left to training, and is typically achieved only with
large volumes of training data.

When the inputs are timestamped observations rather than ordered tokens, however, 
there is actually considerable precedent for the encoding to be concatenated%
~\cite{xu2019functional,horn2020seft}. Nagesh~\emph{et al.}~\cite{nagesh2021ema} 
compare the two arrangements on sequences of timestamped records and find concatenation 
ahead in every setting they test, by a margin that narrows as they add training data. With a
corpus of a few thousand scans, we opt to concatenate.

The attended frames must now be reduced to a single vector that represents the entire cell. 
To achieve this, we use one learned query attending over them, the pooling of the Set
Transformer~\cite{lee2019set}, and in addition, we concatenate a mean of all attended values.
The mean reports the cell as a whole, while the query is free to weight a few frames above 
the rest. 

Two exact summaries join the pooled vector: the first is 16 aggregates taken
over every frame the cell held, including those the cap of 512 dropped.
Table~\ref{tab:inputs} lists the quantities that are aggregated here.
Such counts and totals cannot be reported by the pooled set of vectors 
and are thus directly given to the model. The second summary belongs to channel 
cells alone: the 109 carrier-sense samples of the dwell, one per millisecond, 
read by a strided one-dimensional convolution. (One CCA sample is lost to 
radio retune.)

A final MLP maps the concatenation of the summaries and the pooled vector 
to a single 64 dimensional vector leaving the encoder (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). 
Both an AP and a channel cell share the frame embedding and the self-attention,
but each keeps its own pooling query and its own output MLP, since only 
channel cells carry CCA signals, and an aggregate such as beacon
level does not mean the same thing on the two and should not be afforded the same 
mapping. A cell that heard nothing has nothing to pool and takes a learned 
vector in place of both reductions.

The resulting cell vector, although containing a summary of all its frames, mostly
lacks identifiers to distinguish itself or relate to other cells. We thus add tags
for the channel it was heard on, whether it is an AP or channel cell, and a positional
embedding for the dwell number it belongs to. Each tag is a 64 dimensional vector added onto the cell
vector, its direction drawn once from a random orthogonal set and never
trained. Tags from different sets are orthogonal, as are the tags within the
cell type and channel sets. The dwell tags are instead sinusoidal in the dwell
index, so that dwells close in time take similar tags. 
All tags have a trainable length shared within their kind, initially 0.5. 
Finally the AP's identity must be added to the representation of AP cells, 
for which we take two approaches:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Declare one AP as the target AP whose throughput we are interested in.
  We now code the identity relative to this AP: \code{TARGET\_AP} for the target's
  own cells, \code{CO\_CHANNEL\_AP} for an AP sharing its channel, and
  \code{OTHER\_AP} for the rest. These three roles use learned vectors.
  Inference runs once per target AP,
  with a different set of roles added to the cell vectors each time.

  \item Assign each AP in the scan a numerical identity and look up a fixed
  tag direction. The direction is shared by that AP's cells and its readout row;
  its length is learned. Identities are randomly relabelled for each training
  sample, so the model cannot attach a persistent meaning to an AP number.
  The table fixes the maximum number of APs handled at once, but permits all 
  their throughputs to be predicted from one grid encoding.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Backbone}
\label{sec:backbone}

The tagged cell vectors attend across the scan so that a cell describing one
AP can incorporate the activity of other APs and channels. We consider two
backbones for this step.

The first is a transformer encoder trained from scratch, with two blocks of
four-head self-attention at width 64. Each block normalises its input before
attention, adds the attended values to the input, then applies a feed-forward
MLP with another normalisation and residual connection; that MLP widens to 256
and back. Each block also adds to its attention logits a learned scalar per
head for the channel distance between the two cells, in 20 MHz steps. Every
cell can attend to every observed cell; dwell order is supplied by the tags.

The second follows the adaptation of a pretrained language model described
above. Linear projections map the cell vectors from 64 to 2048 dimensions,
then back after Llama-3.2-1B has processed them. The backbone weights remain
frozen, with LoRA adapters of rank 8 and $\alpha = 32$ on the query and value
projections, at dropout 0.05. Its
attention mask is block causal, permitting cells to attend within their own dwell 
and to earlier dwells. Cells in the same dwell receive the same rotary position, 
since their order within that dwell carries no meaning~\cite{su2024roformer}.

The target-AP model runs the backbone separately for each AP, because changing
the target changes the role tags on its input. The joint-AP model runs it once
for the scan. AP identity tags in this model are assigned randomly within each
sample and shared between an AP's cells and its readout; they provide a way to
match observations without identifying an AP across deployments.

\subsection{Decoder}
\label{sec:decoder}

The backbone returns one vector per cell, while we need one prediction per AP.
To obtain it, we construct a single learnable query per AP. (In the target model, 
this is just one AP)

We provide additional context to the query, by means of an embedded representation
of what could identify the AP. 

We take the AP's channel tag, the AP's identity tag (applicable only to the joint model), 
and an AP descriptor: a whole-window summary of its beacons and its BSS (Exact quantities listed 
in Table~\ref{tab:inputs}.) and project it to 64 dimensions with a two-layer MLP. 

The queries thus created pass through two decoder blocks. Each block lets the queries 
attend to the backbone's cell vectors, using the rows as queries and the cells as keys
and values. The block ends with a standard feed-forward MLP. Both operations have normalisation
and residual connections. In the joint model, self-attention between AP query rows
precedes cross-attention, allowing each prediction to use what the other rows
have learned. The target model has only one query row so it omits this step.

A final MLP maps each row to a mean and log variance of standardised
$\log(1+y_i)$, where $y_i$ is throughput in Mbit/s. Training begins with squared
error on the mean and then uses Gaussian negative log likelihood to fit both
outputs. The predicted means order the APs for selection.

\section{Architecture Choices}
\label{sec:choices}

\subsection{Frame Time and Cell Identity}

We concatenate time embeddings with frame content, but \textit{add} dwell embedding to
cell content following common practice. Concatenation is motivated through the separation of
content and time, but this argument actually needs further qualification. 

The output of attention layers can write context into the timestamp coordinates 
as well as the content coordinates, so even though residual addition retains 
the original input, the fields end up becoming a combined representation nonetheless 
in subsequent layers.

To avoid this problem, TUPE~\cite{ke2021tupe} maintains separation in the 
attention scores themselves. It computes content--content and position--position 
scores with separate projections, then sums them before a single softmax.
The positional encoding remains separate from the contextual content across layers. 
Concatenation alone does not impose this structure: its query and key projections can produce
mixed content--time scores even in the first layer.

The empirical precedent survives this qualification. Nagesh~\emph{et al.}~\cite{nagesh2021ema}
concatenate time with observations in a standard six-layer encoder, without
TUPE's separate positional scores, and still find concatenation ahead of
addition. Our frame encoder has only one self-attention layer before pooling,
so the argument that subsequent layers have greater mixing of fields doesn't really apply.

At the cell level, the temporal coordinate is a dwell number, advancing at
fixed 110\,ms intervals. Frame timestamps instead describe observations that
can cluster in busy periods and become sparse in quiet ones, and thus can be 
treated as true timestamps whereas dwell numbers are closer to a numbered position. 
Nagesh's timestamped-record precedent therefore applies more closely to frames. 
There is also a difference in what the content must represent: 
one frame vector just describes one frame of 10 headers, while
a cell vector summarises all of its frames and their aggregates. This means that dedicating
dimensions to positional embedding comes at a great cost at the cell level, which could better be used
for representational power. Thus, the arguments that support concatenation at the frame level, do not
apply strongly at the cell level, and addition remains the norm.

\subsection{Identity in the Attention Logits}

Alongside the channel tags, we give channel separation an independent
contribution to the attention score. For one head, the logit between query
$i$ and key $j$ becomes
\begin{equation}
 \ell_{ij} = \frac{q_i^\top k_j}{\sqrt{d_h}} + b(\Delta_{ij}),
\end{equation}
where $d_h$ is the query and key dimension, $\Delta_{ij}$ is the separation
between their channels, and $b$ assigns a learned scalar to each possible
separation. Thus, pairs with the same channel separation share a bias,
regardless of their content. Each head learns its own biases.

In encoder self-attention, the channels belong to the query cell and the key
cell. In decoder cross-attention, they belong to the AP being scored and the
key cell; the decoder also adds yet another learned bias for same AP relations. 
These biases adjust the logits before softmax, changing the relative
attention weights while leaving every observed cell available.

T5~\cite{raffel2020t5} and Swin~\cite{liu2021swin} use learned relative-position
biases in attention, while Graphormer~\cite{ying2021graphormer} uses biases
indexed by shortest-path distance between nodes. These provide precedent for
adjusting a query--key score using a relationship between the two inputs.
However, in our case, channel and AP relationships are also available through the tags, 
which raises the question of whether a direct bias supplies anything attention
could not already learn.

To see the overlap, write an input as content plus tag, $x_i=c_i+t_i$.
Let $W_Q$ and $W_K$ be one head's query and key maps. Temporarily omitting
normalisation, projection biases and the attention scale, set
$M=W_Q^\top W_K$. Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 q_i^\top k_j ={}& c_i^\top M c_j + c_i^\top M t_j \\
                 &+ t_i^\top M c_j + t_i^\top M t_j.
\end{split}
\label{eq:tag_attention}
\end{equation}
The last term can express preferences for pairs of tags, including matching
channels or AP identities. In cross-attention the same expansion applies with
AP row content and tags on the query side and cell content and tags on the
key side.

However, $M$ is shared by all four terms. Adjusting it to obtain a particular
tag preference can also change how content is compared and how content and
tags interact. The tag score is therefore tied to the rest of the attention
calculation, even when the tag directions themselves are learned. A direct
bias gives each relationship its own parameter,
adjustable without changing the query and key projections, and shares that
parameter across every pair with the same relationship. Our blocks also
normalise content and tags together, so the expansion above illustrates the
overlap rather than an exact decomposition of their logits. We retain the
channel-distance bias in the encoder and both the channel-distance and own-AP
biases in the decoder to give these relationships an independently adjustable
contribution alongside the tags.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We compare the transformers with passive-selection heuristics and predictors
that read whole-window summaries. We use five-fold cross-validation by
topology: each rotation uses three folds for training, one for validation and
one for testing. Over the five rotations, every topology is tested exactly
once, with all its scans kept together. The LoRA and ranking-loss comparisons
use fold 0 only.

\subsection{Baselines}

We evaluate four reference rules over the discovered APs. \emph{Random choice}
selects uniformly; its scores are the exact expected Top-1 rate and regret,
rather than the outcome of one random draw. \emph{Strongest RSSI} selects the
AP with the highest mean decoded-beacon RSSI. \emph{Least busy channel}
selects an AP whose channel has the lowest CCA busy fraction, averaged over
the dwells on that channel. Tied choices share selection credit and regret
uniformly.

\emph{RSSI minus busy} combines these measurements, scoring AP $i$ as
$r_i-kb_i$, where $r_i$ is its mean beacon RSSI in dBm and $b_i$ is its
channel's CCA busy fraction. We fit the single coefficient $k$ on training
scans, choosing from $\{0,5,\ldots,80\}$ the value with the lowest mean regret.
At $k=0$ this reduces to strongest-RSSI selection.

The tabular predictors read 32 whole-window features per discovered AP:
10 summarise its channel, 16 its own BSS, and 6 its relationship to the other
discovered APs. These include CCA occupancy, frame and byte rates, retries,
beacon RSSI and spacing, observed transmitter counts, and relative signal
strength and airtime shares. They provide a comparison with both linear and
nonlinear fits of summary statistics.

\emph{Ridge regression} fits a linear predictor after standardising the
features on training data, with an $L_2$ penalty selected from
$\alpha\in\{0.1,1,10,100\}$. \emph{Random forest} averages 400 regression
trees; we vary the minimum leaf size over $\{1,2,4\}$ and the number of
features considered at each split between the square root of the feature
count, half the features, and all features. \emph{Histogram gradient boosting}
fits trees sequentially to correct prediction errors. We try 200 or 400
iterations, learning rates in $\{0.03,0.06,0.1\}$, and minimum leaf sizes in
$\{4,8,16\}$, with $L_2$ regularisation fixed at 1.

Each tabular configuration is fitted against either throughput or
$\log(1+y_i)$. Validation mean regret selects the configuration and target
transform within each family, with Spearman correlation breaking ties. The
test topologies are held out from both fitting and selection.

\subsection{Transformer Training and Objectives}

The custom grid encoder contains 100{,}000 parameters across its two blocks;
including the embedding and readout layers, the target-AP and joint-AP models
have 0.313 and 0.347 million trainable parameters. Llama-3.2-1B retains
973 million frozen parameters after removing its unused vocabulary embedding.
Its rank-8 LoRA adapters contain 0.852 million trainable parameters; together
with the width projections, embedding and readout layers, the target and joint
variants train 1.330 and 1.363 million parameters, respectively.

We train the transformer models for 60 epochs with AdamW and cosine
learning-rate decay. The custom models use an initial learning rate of
$10^{-3}$ and weight decay of $10^{-2}$; the LLM variants use $10^{-4}$ for
both. Throughput predictors minimise Gaussian negative log likelihood on
standardised $\log(1+y_i)$, averaged over the discovered APs in each batch.
The first five epochs fit the mean with squared error before introducing
the predicted variance.

The ranking runs instead train the joint-AP model to produce scores $s_i$
using a listwise cross-entropy loss. For one scan, let $z_i$ be the
standardised $\log(1+y_i)$ label. For $T>0$, the target distribution $q_i$
and predicted distribution $p_i$ are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
q_i(T)&=\frac{\exp(z_i/T)}{\sum_j\exp(z_j/T)},
&p_i&=\frac{\exp(s_i)}{\sum_j\exp(s_j)},\\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rank}}&=-\sum_i q_i(T)\log p_i.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ranking_loss}
\end{equation}
The sums run over the scan's discovered APs. A smaller target temperature
$T$ concentrates weight on higher-throughput choices; at $T=0$, only the
best AP receives weight, shared uniformly if several tie. We average this
loss over scans with at least two discovered APs. Ranking runs use no
squared-error warm-up, and their outputs are scores rather than throughput
estimates. For both objectives, validation regret selects the checkpoint,
with Spearman correlation breaking ties.

On fold 0, the target-AP model's mean regret is 0.465\,Mbit/s with the custom
encoder and 0.462\,Mbit/s with the LLM backbone; the joint-AP figures are
0.435 and 0.485\,Mbit/s. Their Top-1 rates lie between 0.885 and 0.891.
The LLM therefore gives comparable selection performance on this fold,
while using about four times as many trainable parameters and a much larger
frozen backbone. Its evaluation is limited to fold 0.

Across five folds, the joint-AP model achieves the lowest mean regret,
0.422\,Mbit/s, compared with 0.444 for target AP and 0.448 for gradient
boosting. Both transformers improve substantially on ridge regression at
1.031\,Mbit/s, but remain close to the nonlinear tabular predictors.

LoRA allows the pretrained backbone to be reused while its adapters and task
layers are fitted to another task or to deployment data. This motivates the
LLM beyond its present selection scores, although transfer to real captures
is untested and the custom models can also be fine-tuned.

Block-causal attention also allows the LLM to encode a scan prefix without
access to later dwells. A shorter scan requires determining the discovered
APs and their summaries from the observed prefix. The custom encoder also
accepts fewer cells, although its bidirectional cell representations change when later
cells are added. One-second scan prefixes have not been evaluated, so their
selection accuracy remains an open question.

Gradient boosting's close performance may reflect the fixed mean offered
rates during each scan: arrivals and contention fluctuate, but traffic
sources do not change their mean loads or start and stop during observation.
Window summaries may therefore capture much of the information needed for
selection. Varying loads and bursty sessions would test the value of temporal
modelling; we will discuss these extensions in Section~\ref{sec:future}.
